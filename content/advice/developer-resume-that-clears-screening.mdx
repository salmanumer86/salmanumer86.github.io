I read developer résumés for a living, and I'll be honest: most of them get a "no" in under a minute. Not because the person is a bad engineer, but because the document doesn't let me find out.

Here's what actually gets a CV through screening.

## Lead with what you built, not what you know

"Skills: React, Redux, Node, MongoDB, Docker, AWS, Git" tells me nothing. Every CV has that list.

Instead, under each role, write two or three lines that follow this shape:

> Built the checkout flow for a Shopify-style store in Next.js and Stripe. Reduced cart abandonment by 18% by moving validation client-side.

Now I know what you built, what stack you used to build it, and that you measured the result. That's three signals in one sentence.

## Put links at the top

GitHub, a deployed app, a portfolio — put them in the header, under your name. I open the links *before* I read the bullet points. If they're buried at the bottom, I might never get there.

If your work is all on private repos, say so and describe one thing in enough detail that I could ask you a follow-up question about it.

## One page for under five years, two for more

Nobody reads page three. If you're cutting content, cut the oldest role first, then the "Hobbies" section, then the summary paragraph nobody asked for.

## The five reasons I say no in under a minute

1. **No dates, or dates that don't add up.** Gaps are fine. Hidden gaps look like something else.
2. **A skills list with 40 items.** I assume you're at a beginner level in most of them.
3. **"Worked on" instead of "built".** Tell me what you owned.
4. **No links and no detail.** I have nothing to verify.
5. **The same bullets under three different jobs.** Copy-paste is visible.

## What I don't care about

- A photo (unless the role is customer-facing)
- Your marital status, date of birth or religion
- An "Objective" section
- Fancy templates. A clean Google Doc is fine.
