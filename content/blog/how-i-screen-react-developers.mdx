Most tech recruiters screen on keywords because that's all they can do. If the CV says *React*, *Next.js* and *TypeScript*, it goes in the "yes" pile. The problem is that everyone who has done a weekend tutorial can write those three words.

I write React alongside the recruiting, so I screen differently. This is the process.

## 1. The CV is a table of contents, not the book

I read the CV to find out *where to look*, not to make a decision. What I'm noting:

- Which projects they say they owned, versus contributed to
- Whether the stack they list is consistent with the roles they held
- Any public links: GitHub, a deployed app, a portfolio, npm packages

A CV with no links isn't a red flag — plenty of good engineers work on private codebases. But it means the next step matters more.

## 2. I read the repo

If there's a GitHub profile, I open it. I'm not grading architecture; I'm looking for a handful of concrete signals:

| Signal | What it tells me |
| --- | --- |
| Commit history over months, not one big "initial commit" | They actually built it, incrementally |
| Component boundaries that make sense | They've felt the pain of a 900-line component |
| Some tests, even a few | They know what breaks |
| A README that explains how to run it | They've handed work to other people |

Ten minutes in a repo tells me more than thirty minutes of CV.

## 3. The follow-up question

On the screening call, I ask about one specific thing they built. Then I ask a second question about the same thing.

> "You mentioned you moved data fetching into a server component. What did that break?"

Someone who did the work has an answer — usually a slightly annoyed one, because they remember the bug. Someone who is repeating a buzzword stalls. That second question is where most of the value in screening sits.

<Callout>
I won't pretend I can grade a senior engineer's architecture decisions. What I can do is make sure you're not spending interview time on someone who can't answer a follow-up.
</Callout>

## 4. What I hand over

For every candidate on a shortlist you get:

1. A two-paragraph summary in plain English — what they've built, what I'd probe in the interview
2. Links to the specific repos or deployments I looked at
3. Salary expectations and notice period, confirmed, so there are no surprises at offer stage

## Why this matters more than it sounds

The cost of a bad hire isn't the salary. It's the three months your senior engineers spend onboarding someone who then leaves, and the feature that slipped while they did it. A tighter shortlist is the cheapest insurance you can buy.
